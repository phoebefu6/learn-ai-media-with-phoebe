Why this is the last session and not an appendix

The law did not get delayed. You just were not told

3 min live

There is a widespread belief that the EU pushed its AI transparency rules back. It did not. The part that governs what you publish has been in force since the start of this month, and the confusion has a specific cause worth naming.

EU AI Act Article 50 is live. It has applied since 2 August 2026. The Digital Omnibus, Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 of 8 July 2026, moved the high-risk deadlines out to December 2027 and August 2028. It did not touch Article 50. Every headline you read about the AI Act being delayed was about a different set of obligations entirely.

That matters because Article 50 is the one article in the whole regulation that speaks directly to a person making a video. It does not care whether your system is high risk. It cares whether a viewer can tell.

LiveThe one real carve-out, and the date it expires2 min▶

There is exactly one genuine grace period, and it is narrower than people hope.

  • Generative systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026 must comply with the Article 50(2) marking obligation only from 2 December 2026. That is a provider-side extension on machine-readable marking for tools that already existed.
  • It does not extend your own duty. The deployer obligation in 50(4) has no such grace period. If you publish a deep fake today, the disclosure duty applies today.

There is also a voluntary layer sitting next to the law. The Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-generated Content, published 10 June 2026, had roughly 190 signatory organisations by late July 2026. Voluntary means voluntary. Signing it is a signal, not a defence, and not signing it is not a violation.

So the working position for tonight is this. The obligation on you is already running, the obligation on your tools finishes phasing in on 2 December 2026, and by the time anyone gets round to enforcing either one you will already have published the thing.

Part 1 · covers who Article 50 actually names

Two duties, and only one of them is yours

6 min live

Article 50 splits cleanly in half. One half is a duty on the people who build the model. The other half is a duty on the person who presses publish. Almost everybody reads the first half and assumes it covers them.

Read the right-hand panel again. The deployer is the person putting the content in front of an audience. That is you, whether you generated the footage yourself or commissioned it, whether you are a company or a person with a phone. The provider duty in 50(2) is real and it is being built into the tools, but it does not discharge yours.

The artistic carve-out is the part people over-read. It is a reduced obligation, not an exemption. The regulation says disclosure for evidently artistic, creative, satirical, fictional or analogous work must not hamper the display or enjoyment of the work. That is an instruction about placement and proportion, not permission to say nothing. A credit at the end of a short film satisfies it. A film with no mention anywhere does not.

Self-studyWhy "deep fake" is a wider word than you think3 min▶

The word carries baggage from politics, and the baggage causes people to assume it does not apply to them.

The 50(4) duty attaches to content that has been artificially generated or manipulated in a way that resembles real people, places or events. A synthetic presenter reading your product update is inside that description. So is a voice clone of your CEO reading a script the CEO never read, even if the CEO approved it. Consent from the person depicted is not the same thing as disclosure to the viewer, and Article 50(4) is about the viewer.

What is outside it is the ordinary invisible use of AI in production. Cutting silence, cleaning up audio, generating captions, colour work. Nothing there resembles a real person doing something they did not do, so nothing there triggers a deep fake disclosure. That distinction, generation of apparent reality versus assistance with real footage, is the line every platform policy in the next section is also trying to draw, with varying success.

Part 2 · covers what provenance actually proves

A Content Credential is a claim, not a verdict

7 min live

C2PA is the standard everyone points at when asked how we will know what is real. It is genuinely good engineering. It also does far less than the people citing it believe, and the most honest description of its limits is published by C2PA itself.

Take the strongest sentences one at a time, because their source is what makes them usable. From the Security Considerations document: "C2PA does not offer any protection against the complete removal of C2PA manifests from assets." Screenshot the video, re-encode it, re-render it, and the credential is gone. Nothing about the pixels changed. The provenance did.

From the Harms Modelling document, two lines that should end most arguments in most meeting rooms. The presence of valid manifests does not mean that anything is true, and assets can carry valid C2PA manifests and still be disinformation. And the mirror image, which matters more in practice: "The fact that any digital asset does not have Content Credentials does not mean that its contents are not to be trusted."

And from the specification's own scope, C2PA should not provide value judgments about whether a given set of provenance data is good or bad, only whether the assertions inside it validate, are correctly formed and are free from tampering. That is a standards body telling you, in its normative text, not to use it as a truth signal.

The one sentence to carry out of this session. A Content Credential attests to a signed claim about an asset's history. It does not attest that the content is true, accurate, or fairly presented. Anyone who tells you provenance solves misinformation has not read the specification they are citing.

Self-studyThe air-gap attack, admitted in YouTube's own help docs3 min▶

YouTube runs a label reading "Captured with a camera", derived from C2PA data. Its own help page tells you what the label's absence means, and it is refreshingly blunt: if it is missing, that does not mean the content has modified audio or visuals.

The same documentation describes the attack that breaks the label's presence. Point a C2PA-enabled camera at a screen showing synthetic content. The camera did genuinely capture what was in front of it, so it signs a genuine capture credential. The result is fully synthetic footage carrying a valid, honestly issued "captured with a camera" claim.

This is not a bug anyone can patch. It is the boundary of what a cryptographic binding can say: the camera can attest to what reached its sensor, and it cannot attest to what was in the room. Every provenance system built on capture-time signing has this hole, and the reason to teach it is not cynicism. It is so that when a credential is produced as evidence in front of you, you ask the right next question.

Self-studySynthID, the layer that survives what C2PA does not2 min▶

If manifests can be stripped, the obvious complement is a mark that lives in the content rather than beside it. That is what a soft binding is, and SynthID is the largest deployed example.

It embeds a signal directly in the pixels or the audio samples, and it is designed to survive cropping, filters, frame-rate change and lossy compression. Google reported over 10 billion pieces of content watermarked as of May 2025.

Read the two layers together rather than as rivals. The hard binding gives you a cryptographically strong claim that is easy to remove. The soft binding gives you a weaker claim that is hard to remove. Durable Content Credentials are the attempt to combine metadata, watermark and fingerprint so that stripping one does not strip them all. Neither layer, alone or together, tells you whether the content is honest. They tell you how it was made, and only when they survive.

Part 3 · covers what each platform requires of you

Four platforms asking four different questions

7 min live

There is no single disclosure rule and there is no chance of one arriving. Each platform wrote its own trigger, gave you a different control, shows the viewer a different string, and puts it in a different place. Publishing the same video to four places means answering four questions.

Two things fall out of that table immediately. First, none of these is a blanket label-all-AI rule. Every one of them is scoped to something, and the scope is usually realism. Second, the placement differences are not cosmetic. A label on the player and a label three taps into an overflow menu are different products, and one of them is a decision about how much the platform wants the viewer to notice.

Self-studyFour exemptions that surprise people every single time3 min▶

Most disclosure anxiety is spent on things nobody is asking you to disclose. From YouTube's own policy page, no disclosure is required for:

  • Beauty filters. The thing people assume is the most obviously synthetic layer on the internet is explicitly out of scope.
  • Colour adjustment. Grading, correction, look-up tables. Out.
  • Caption creation. Everything session 4 was about generates no disclosure duty at all.
  • Cloning your own voice for your own dubs. This is the one that stops the room. Your voice, your content, another language: no label required.

TikTok adds one of its own. Generic text-to-speech does not require disclosure when the voice is not a recognisable voice of a known individual. The pivot is not synthesis, it is identity. A synthetic narrator is fine. A synthetic narrator that sounds like a specific real person is not.

Notice how closely that tracks Article 50(4). The law and the platforms have converged, from different directions, on the same underlying question: does this content make a viewer believe a real person did something they did not do? Once you hold that question, most of the rules stop needing to be memorised.

Part 4 · covers how these policies actually get written

The video that forced the AI policy was not AI

6 min live

If you only remember one causal chain from this course, make it this one. It explains why platform AI policies look the way they do, and it explains why the first version of Meta's label had to be renamed within three months.

The Oversight Board's phrasing is the part worth memorising, because it is a diagnosis rather than a complaint. A policy inappropriately focused on how content has been created is a policy that asks the wrong question. It asks which tool touched this. The question that matters to a viewer is whether they are being misled.

Meta then walked straight into the consequence of asking the tool question. "Made with AI" launched on 5 April 2024 and was renamed to "AI info" on 1 July 2024 because content with only minor AI modifications, retouching tools and the like, carried industry standard indicators and was being labelled as AI. Real photographs were being flagged as artificial. On 12 September 2024 the placement split followed: AI-generated content keeps a visible label, AI-edited content moves into the post menu.

That is a complete failure loop in five months, and it is not a story about Meta being careless. It is what happens when a detection signal, the C2PA indicator, gets used as an editorial signal. The manifest was doing exactly what it was designed to do, which is to record that a tool touched the file. Meta read that as a claim about the nature of the content, which C2PA's own scope explicitly says it is not.

Self-studyLinkedIn is the contrast case, and it is the better design3 min▶

Set LinkedIn against the other three and the difference is structural, not cosmetic.

Its duty is deception-scoped. Disclosure is required for synthetic media that misrepresents a real person or real events, with an explicit parody carve-out. It is not a blanket label-all obligation. That is the Oversight Board's diagnosis, implemented: the question is about misleading the viewer, not about which tool was used.

Its label is not a label. LinkedIn renders a C2PA provenance panel built from the manifest the upstream tool signed. It is display-only. It does not sign anything, does not assert anything of its own, and if no manifest survived the upload there is nothing to display.

Both halves are honest, and the second one has a sharp edge. A LinkedIn post with no provenance panel tells you nothing whatsoever. That is not a flaw in their implementation, it is the strippability problem from Part 2 showing up in a real product. Which is exactly why the deception-scoped duty has to exist next to it, and why it is doing more work than the panel is.

Self-studyThe US position, in about ninety seconds3 min▶

Three live instruments and one that keeps getting cited as though it were law.

  • TAKE IT DOWN Act. The platform notice-and-removal duty went live on 19 May 2026. Removal as soon as possible and not later than 48 hours, plus reasonable efforts to find known identical copies. Enforced by the FTC. Its digital forgery definition explicitly covers content made using software, machine learning or artificial intelligence.
  • California AI Transparency Act, SB 942 as amended by AB 853, operative 2 August 2026. Above 1,000,000 monthly users: a free public AI detection tool with an API, optional manifest disclosure, mandatory latent disclosure. Large online platforms above 2,000,000 unique monthly users pick up provenance duties from 1 January 2027. Penalty: $5,000 per violation, each day counting separately.
  • The detail that matters for procurement. The California statute does not name C2PA. It says "widely adopted specifications adopted by an established standards-setting body". If a vendor tells you the law requires C2PA specifically, they are selling, not citing.
  • NO FAKES Act is still only a bill. Introduced, not passed. Treat every article describing it as a rule as a warning about that publication.

Note the shape across both jurisdictions. Europe puts the duty on the publisher and the provider. California puts it on the very large platform and the very large developer. Neither of them lets the individual creator off, and neither of them will arrive as a notification in your editing software.

Build-along

Write the disclosure line for the video you have been building

★ 18 min · everyone builds

You have carried the same sixty seconds through five sessions. Tonight it gets the last thing it needs, which is a sentence you would be willing to defend. Work in order. Step 3 is the one that decides everything after it.

List every AI touch in the file, honestly. Silence cutting, filler removal, captions, generated b-roll, voice clone, upscale, colour. Write them down even if you are certain they are exempt. You cannot reason about a list you have not made.

Cross off everything that is assistance rather than apparent reality. Caption generation, colour adjustment, beauty filters, cloning your own voice for your own dub. These are named exemptions on YouTube's own policy page, and they sit outside Article 50(4) too because nothing there depicts a real person doing what they did not do.

Ask the one question that survives every jurisdiction. Does anything left on the list make a viewer believe a real person said or did something they did not? If yes, you have a 50(4) duty and a platform duty, and the rest of this is about wording. If no, you may still choose to disclose, and that is now a taste decision rather than a legal one.

Write the sentence at the length the work can carry. One line, plain, in the voice of the piece. If the work is evidently artistic, creative, satirical or fictional, the obligation is reduced and must not hamper display or enjoyment, so an end credit is enough. If it is presented as a record of something real, it goes where a viewer meets it, not in the description.

Set the platform control on every destination separately. YouTube: Studio, Attributes, "AI use". TikTok: the AIGC label, or your own caption or sticker. Meta: the AI-disclosure tool. LinkedIn: no tool exists, so your disclosure has to live in the post copy. Four destinations, four actions.

Test what survives. Export, screenshot the export, and inspect both for a manifest. Whatever the screenshot lost is what a re-upload will lose. This is the two-minute version of the Part 2 lesson, and it is the one that makes it stick.

Why the disclosure line is written last but decided first. If you cannot write the sentence without it sounding like an apology, the problem is usually upstream in the edit rather than in the wording. A piece that is honest about what it is does not find this sentence hard. Take the ten minutes here rather than the ten minutes arguing about it later.

Homework

After the last session

30 min
  1. Read Article 50 yourself, paragraphs 2 and 4 only. It is two paragraphs and it takes six minutes. Find the words "machine-readable" in one and "deep fake" in the other, and notice which paragraph names you. Reading the primary text once is worth more than reading ten explainers about it.
  2. Open C2PA's Harms Modelling document and find the sentence about absent credentials. Then decide, in writing, what you will say the next time someone in a meeting treats a missing credential as evidence of anything.
  3. Publish one thing to two platforms and compare what the viewer sees. Same file, same disclosure, two destinations. Screenshot both. The gap between them is the thing this session exists to make visible.
  4. Audit one piece of your own back catalogue against the exemption list. Find one video you worried about disclosing that you did not need to, and one you did not think about that you probably did. Both exist. That asymmetry is the normal state.
  5. Optional, and it is the most useful thing on this list: take any article claiming the EU delayed its AI transparency rules, open Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, and find out which deadlines actually moved. Then notice how many people you know believe the headline.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and the last stamp goes in your passport.

1 · You publish a video containing a synthetic presenter. Under EU AI Act Article 50, whose duty is it to disclose that to the viewer?

50(2) is a provider duty about machine-readable marking, and it does not discharge yours. 50(4) names the deployer, which is whoever puts the content in front of an audience. The reduced obligation for evidently artistic, creative, satirical or fictional work is an instruction about placement, disclosure must not hamper display or enjoyment, not a permission to say nothing. And the 2 December 2026 date applies only to the 50(2) marking duty for systems placed on the market before 2 August 2026.

2 · A clip arrives carrying a valid Content Credential. What has that established?

C2PA's own scope says it should not provide value judgments about whether provenance data is good or bad, only whether the assertions validate. Harms Modelling adds that valid manifests do not mean anything is true, and that assets can carry them and still be disinformation. B overcorrects: strippability limits what an absent credential can prove, not what a present one proves. C2PA is equally clear that missing Content Credentials do not mean the contents are not to be trusted.

3 · Meta's AI labelling policy exists because a specific video went to the Oversight Board. What was in that video?

Case 2023-029-FB-UA, decided 5 February 2024, concerned an altered video that was a looped edit of real footage. The Board called the policy incoherent, lacking in persuasive justification and inappropriately focused on how content has been created, and Meta adopted labelling on 5 April 2024. B and C are what everyone assumes, and the assumption is the point: the policy governing AI content was forced by content that was not AI, which is why it asked the wrong question and had to be renamed within three months.
